**The two hypotheses being tested**

- **H1**: US manufacturing has declined; new plants are built by foreign manufacturers entering under tariff and reshoring pressure and run already automated, AI-applied systems, so there is little place to deploy field data experts.
- **H2**: the shop floor exists and lacks people; the federal share is small because workforce training is institutionally left to states, community colleges and private firms.

## Key Question 1. Size and trend of US manufacturing: what declined and what did not

### Takeaway
The share of manufacturing in GDP and in employment has fallen for decades, but the absolute base is large: about $2.9 trillion of value added in 2024 (second in the world after China), about 12.65 million payroll employees and about 239,000 firms. The collapse is concentrated in labour-intensive sectors (apparel, textiles, furniture); output in computers and electronics, motor vehicles and aerospace rose.

### Cited Findings
- Real manufacturing value added was about $2.4 trillion in 2025:Q3, about 9.5 percent of US GDP, the third-largest private sector; the share was about 25 percent in the mid-twentieth century. [P2, Cleveland Fed citing BEA] — [Cleveland Fed Economic Commentary 2026-21, 2026-09-21](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/ec-202621-trends-in-manufacturing-in-the-united-states)
- 2024: manufacturing value added $2.4 trillion in chained 2017 dollars, 10.0 % of GDP; direct plus indirect contribution an estimated 15.4 % of GDP. [P2, NIST citing BEA] — [NIST, Manufacturing Economy](https://www.nist.gov/el/applied-economics-office/manufacturing/total-us-manufacturing/manufacturing-economy)
- 2024, current dollars: US $2.9 trillion of manufacturing value added, China $4.7 trillion; the US is the second-largest manufacturing nation; China produces more than the US in 11 of 14 subsectors; the US 5-year compound growth rate ranks 129th. [P2, NIST citing UN Statistics Division] — [NIST, Total US Manufacturing](https://www.nist.gov/el/applied-economics-office/manufacturing/manufacturing-economy/total-us-manufacturing); [NIST, Manufacturing Economy](https://www.nist.gov/el/applied-economics-office/manufacturing/total-us-manufacturing/manufacturing-economy)
- 2023: the US accounted for roughly 15 percent of global manufacturing value added, second to China. [P2, Cleveland Fed citing UNIDO Yearbook 2024] — [Cleveland Fed EC 2026-21](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/ec-202621-trends-in-manufacturing-in-the-united-states)
- 2024 value added by subsector (chained 2017 dollars): chemical products $498.1B; computer and electronic products $297.3B; food, beverage and tobacco $292B; motor vehicles and parts $220.5B; other transportation equipment $190.1B; machinery $141.1B; ... textile mills $13.6B; apparel and leather $11.5B. [P2, NIST citing BEA] — [NIST, Total US Manufacturing](https://www.nist.gov/el/applied-economics-office/manufacturing/manufacturing-economy/total-us-manufacturing)
- Real manufacturing output grew about 1.6 percent a year between 1998 and 2023; industrial production in manufacturing "has largely stalled over the past decade"; manufacturing total factor productivity has contracted since the mid-2000s while the private economy's rose. [P2] — [Cleveland Fed EC 2026-21](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/ec-202621-trends-in-manufacturing-in-the-united-states)

[P2, a data-visualisation site; underlying source not checked, so "확인 필요"] — [Voronoi, 2025-04-17](https://www.voronoiapp.com/economy/-Since-2000-Most-US-Manufacturing-Sectors-ContractedExcept-High-Tech-and-Transportation-4760)
- Employment share: about 25 percent of the workforce in 1976, roughly 10 percent in June 2025. The decline is driven mainly by fewer young workers entering, not by higher exits. [P2] — [Cleveland Fed EC 2026-21](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/ec-202621-trends-in-manufacturing-in-the-united-states)
- Payroll employment: 12,652,000 in September 2026 (+9,000 from August); 2017–2019 average 12,613,000. [P2, NAM citing BLS; page updated 2026-10-13] — [NAM, Facts About Manufacturing](https://nam.org/mfgdata/facts-about-manufacturing-expanded/)
- A different measure: 15.1 million employees in 2025 per the Current Population Survey, 9.3 % of total employment. The household survey counts more people than the payroll survey; the two must not be mixed. [P2, NIST] — [NIST, Manufacturing Economy](https://www.nist.gov/el/applied-economics-office/manufacturing/total-us-manufacturing/manufacturing-economy)
- Recent direction of employment: an estimated 103,000 manufacturing jobs lost between January 2025 and January 2026; 4.702 million fewer manufacturing jobs in January 2026 than in January 2000. [S] — [Manufacturing Dive](https://www.manufacturingdive.com/news/bls-manufacturing-jobs-loss-april-jolts-march-2026-first-brand-group/819770/); [manufacturingleadgeneration.com](https://manufacturingleadgeneration.com/us-manufacturing-employment-history/)
- Firms: 239,265 manufacturing firms in 2022; all but 4,177 have fewer than 500 employees; about three-quarters have fewer than 20; 93.1% have fewer than 100; but 59.1% of employees work for firms with 500 or more. [P2, NAM citing Census] — [NAM, Facts About Manufacturing](https://nam.org/mfgdata/facts-about-manufacturing-expanded/)
- Establishments grew more than 11% between 2019:Q1 and 2023:Q2, approaching 393,000. [P2, Deloitte citing BLS QCEW] — [Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute, 2024](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/manufacturing-industrial-products/supporting-us-manufacturing-growth-amid-workforce-challenges.html)

### Inferences
- "Declined" is true for shares, for employment (about 17 million in 2000 to 12.65 million) and for specific labour-intensive sectors. It is not true for the absolute level of value added, and not for the number of establishments in 2019–2023. The H1 premise holds for employment and share and fails for output and plant count.
- The base is dominated numerically by small firms (about 235,000 firms under 500 employees), while most workers are in large firms. Whether a "shop floor to deploy experts into" exists is therefore two different questions: many small plants, and a smaller number of large ones.

### Gaps
- BLS employment by 3-digit NAICS sector (the exact size of sector-level job losses) could not be read: `bls.gov` and FRED were unreachable. The −85%/−64%/−45% figures above are production indices from an unverified secondary site.
- UNIDO and World Bank tables were not opened; the world share (about 15%, 2023) is via the Cleveland Fed.
- Census ASM/CBP establishment counts were not read directly (API key required).

### Inferences
- The evidence contradicts "little place to deploy field experts" in the foreign plants: they need process engineers and installation specialists in large numbers. ## Key Question 4. Labour-shortage evidence

### Takeaway
Survey and vacancy data show unfilled demand: about 481,000 manufacturing openings in June 2026, more than one-fifth of plants citing insufficient labour as a reason for running below capacity, and 72.1% of NAM respondents needing skilled production workers. The large headline gaps (1.9 million, 67,000) are projections by industry bodies, and payroll employment fell during 2025.

### Cited Findings
- 2025:Q3 Quarterly Survey of Plant Capacity Utilization: "more than one-fifth of all manufacturers indicate that they were not able to work at full production capacity because of an insufficient supply of labor". [P2, Cleveland Fed citing Census] — [Cleveland Fed EC 2026-21](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/ec-202621-trends-in-manufacturing-in-the-united-states)
- Same survey through 2024:Q3: about 20.6% of plants below full capacity cited insufficient labour or labour skills; roughly 20% in the late 1990s, below 5% at a later point. [S] — [Supply Chain Management Review](https://www.scmr.com/article/labor-shortages-remain-an-ongoing-concern-in-many-parts-of-u.s-manufacturing)
- Across industries, new import protection is positively and significantly correlated with the share of plants reporting insufficient labour. [P2, figure description only] — [Federal Reserve FEDS Note, 2025-10-31](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/can-tariffs-spur-higher-factory-floor-utilization-accessible-20251031.htm)
- NAM Q4 2025 survey: hiring needs are skilled production workers (technicians, welders, machinists) 72.1%; core production workers 60.1%; degreed workers (scientists, researchers, engineers) 33.5%. The top business challenges were trade uncertainty 73.1%, health-care costs 70.2% and weak domestic demand 60.1%. [P] — [NAM Q4 2025 Manufacturers' Outlook Survey](https://nam.org/2025-fourth-quarter-manufacturers-outlook-survey/)
- JOLTS: manufacturing job openings 481,000 in June 2026, about 23% above a year earlier; revised May 2026 517,000 openings, 296,000 hires, 301,000 separations; June quits 192,000, layoffs and discharges 94,000. [P2] — [Manufacturing Dive, August 2026](https://www.manufacturingdive.com/news/bls-manufacturing-july-employee-situation-june-jolts-2026/827267/)
- Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute (published April 2024; study begun December 2023; online survey of more than 200 US manufacturers plus more than 10 executive interviews): "The net need for new employees in manufacturing could be around 3.8 million between 2024 and 2033. And, around half of these open jobs (1.9 million) could remain unfilled if manufacturers are not able to address the skills gap and the applicant gap". [P] — [Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/manufacturing-industrial-products/supporting-us-manufacturing-growth-amid-workforce-challenges.html)
- Composition of the 3.8 million: 2.8 million from retirements, 760,000 from industry growth, 230,000 from recent legislation. [P2, NAM summary of the same study] — [NAM, Facts About Manufacturing](https://nam.org/mfgdata/facts-about-manufacturing-expanded/)
- Same study: statisticians and data scientists were about 7,500 employees in manufacturing in 2022; industrial machinery maintenance technicians over 270,000; mechanical and industrial engineers nearly 370,000; 75% rise in demand for simulation software skills over five years. [P] — [Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/manufacturing-industrial-products/supporting-us-manufacturing-growth-amid-workforce-challenges.html)
- SIA and Oxford Economics (2023): semiconductor workforce to grow by nearly 115,000 jobs by 2030 (about 345,000 to about 460,000); roughly 67,000 risk going unfilled, of which 39% technicians, 35% four-year engineers or computer scientists, 26% master's or PhD engineers. Over 50% of master's and over 60% of PhD engineering graduates at US universities are foreign citizens. [P] — [SIA, Chipping Away](https://www.semiconductors.org/chipping-away-assessing-and-addressing-the-labor-market-gap-facing-the-u-s-semiconductor-industry/)
- Counter-evidence on the nature of the shortage: the manufacturing wage premium turned negative in the early 1990s; workers with a high-school diploma or less have faced negative premia since the late 1980s, while college-educated manufacturing workers keep a positive premium. [P2] — [Cleveland Fed EC 2026-21](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/ec-202621-trends-in-manufacturing-in-the-united-states)
- Counter-evidence on demand: manufacturing lost an estimated 103,000 jobs between January 2025 and January 2026. [S] — [Manufacturing Dive](https://www.manufacturingdive.com/news/bls-manufacturing-jobs-loss-april-jolts-march-2026-first-brand-group/819770/)

### Inferences
- The shortage evidence is strongest for skilled production roles (technicians, machinists, maintenance) and for semiconductor engineers. It is weakest as a count of "data experts": the one figure found puts statisticians and data scientists at about 7,500 of roughly 12.7 million manufacturing workers in 2022.
- Falling payrolls alongside about half a million openings, and a negative wage premium for less-educated workers, are consistent with a shortage at the offered wage and skill mix, not an absolute absence of workers. This qualifies H2 without refuting it.
- The 1.9 million and 67,000 figures come from bodies that represent employers; they are scenario projections conditional on "if not addressed", not measured vacancies.

### Gaps
- The JOLTS manufacturing openings rate and the September 2026 figure (released 2026-11-03) were not read; `bls.gov` returned 403.
- The Reshoring Initiative's own report was not opened; figures are via trade press.
- No estimate found of unfilled demand for manufacturing AI or data roles specifically.

## Key Question 5. Who pays for manufacturing workforce training

### Takeaway
Workforce training in the US is financed mainly outside federal programme budgets. Federal WIOA Title I money (about $2.9 billion to states for programme year 2026) is distributed by formula, is not manufacturing-specific, and mostly funds career services. States run customised training as an investment incentive, employers are the largest spender, and federal manufacturing-extension money is by statute matched one-to-one.

### Cited Findings
- WIOA programme year 2026 appropriations: Youth $948,130,000; Adult $875,649,000; Dislocated Worker $1,396,412,000 (of which $1,095,349,000 to states and $297,321,597 national reserve). ## Key Question 6. Which parts of each hypothesis does the evidence support

### Takeaway
Neither hypothesis survives whole. H1 is right that the share and employment of US manufacturing fell and that flagship greenfield plants bring their own experts, and wrong that the base is small, mostly foreign or already automated. H2 is right that a large, under-automated shop floor reports unfilled skilled jobs and that training finance sits outside federal technology budgets, and it overstates how firmly the shortage figures are established.

### Inferences
- A combined reading fits the evidence better than either hypothesis: (1) a large legacy base, mostly small firms, under-automated and short of skilled trades, whose training is financed by states, colleges and employers; and (2) a minority of foreign megaprojects whose process know-how is supplied by expatriates and vendors, handled through visas and state incentives. In both segments the federal technology budget is not the channel that pays for field people.
- The part of H1 worth keeping is about where expertise sits, not about automation: in the greenfield plants the "field expert" is the foreign parent's employee. The part of H2 worth keeping is the funding architecture.
- A point that cuts against using either hypothesis as an explanation for the budget share: the premise itself (a small workforce share) rests on budget lines without a federal crosscut (see `us_budget_shares.md`), so the thing being explained is only partly measured.

### Gaps
- No source directly tests either causal claim. I found no government or academic document that explains the workforce share of manufacturing-AI spending.
- Plant-level automation data for foreign greenfield sites, a brownfield/greenfield capacity split, and manufacturing-specific training expenditure are all missing, and each would be needed to settle the question.
